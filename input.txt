Chapter 1

The village of Silverbrook sat at the edge of a quiet forest, where the mornings began with mist and the evenings ended with the sound of crickets. Nobody there was in a hurry. The baker opened his shop before sunrise, and the smell of warm bread drifted down every lane. The old clock tower in the square had stopped working years ago, and no one had ever bothered to fix it, because everyone agreed that time moved well enough without it.

Mira was a young girl who lived with her grandfather in a small stone house near the river. Every day after school she walked along the water and looked for smooth, shiny pebbles. She kept them in an old wooden box under her bed, and her grandfather liked to say that the box held more treasure than any king's castle. Each pebble had a name, and each name had a story. There was Pip, who was round and gray, and Captain, who was long and striped, and Button, who was so small that she could hide him in her palm.

One rainy afternoon, Mira found a pebble unlike any other. It was dark blue, and when she held it up to the light, tiny silver lines glowed inside it like stars. She ran home as fast as she could, splashing through every puddle on the way, and showed it to her grandfather, who was sitting by the fire with a cup of tea.

He studied the stone for a long moment and then smiled. "This one has traveled a very long way," he said softly. "It has been carried by rivers, pushed by storms, and waited for years for someone patient enough to find it."

"Where did it come from?" Mira asked.

"Perhaps from the mountains far to the north," he said. "Perhaps from the bottom of the sea. A stone can have a long memory, if you know how to listen."

That night, Mira placed the blue pebble on her windowsill and watched it glow in the moonlight. She named it Starling, and she whispered her day into its silver lines before she fell asleep. In her dreams she followed the river upstream, past the old mill, past the fields of tall grass, and into the green shadows of the forest where she had never been allowed to go alone.

The next morning, the mist was thicker than usual. Mira ate her breakfast quickly, tucked Starling into her coat pocket, and walked to the edge of the forest. She stood there for a while, listening to the birds and feeling the cool air on her face. A narrow path wound between the trees, covered in soft brown leaves. She took one step, then another, and soon the village was hidden behind her.

The forest was full of small wonders. A family of deer paused to look at her and then slipped away without a sound. A woodpecker tapped out a slow rhythm on an old oak, and a stream giggled over the rocks beside the path. Mira walked on, and every time she felt a little afraid, she touched the stone in her pocket and felt its steady warmth.

After a while she came to a clearing where a very old woman sat on a fallen log, weaving a basket from long green reeds. The woman had silver hair tied in a braid, and her hands moved as quickly as swallows.

"Good morning, little one," said the woman, without looking up. "You are far from home."

"I am looking for where my stone came from," Mira said. She took Starling from her pocket and held it out.

The old woman set down her reeds and held the pebble to the light. Her eyes grew bright. "Ah, I know this one," she said. "It fell from a mountain many years ago, when I was no older than you. I lost it in the river and thought I would never see it again."

Mira's heart sank. "Then it belongs to you," she said, and she held it out a little farther.

But the woman closed Mira's fingers gently around the stone and shook her head. "A stone belongs to whoever treasures it," she said. "I kept it for a little while, and now it has found you. That is how the best things travel, from hand to hand and from heart to heart."

They sat together for the rest of the morning. The woman taught Mira how to weave a small basket from the reeds, and Mira told her all about the village, the baker, the broken clock, and her grandfather's tea. When the sun climbed high above the trees, the woman pointed down the path and told her to go home before the afternoon rain.

Mira walked back through the forest with her new basket in one hand and Starling in the other. When she reached the edge of the trees, she saw her grandfather waiting by the gate, shading his eyes against the light. She ran to him and told him everything, and he listened with a quiet smile.

"You went looking for the beginning of a story," he said, "and you found a new one instead."

From that day on, Mira kept Starling in the basket beside her bed, and she visited the old woman in the clearing every week. She understood now that the best things in life are not found by rushing. They are found by walking slowly, looking carefully, and never giving up on the next small wonder waiting just around the bend.
